## The problem

Affordable multi-axis force sensors are almost always built around a **custom compliant part** —
a flexure that deforms under load. Whatever creep, hysteresis, and temperature drift that part
has then becomes the finished sensor's error budget, and a single calibration can't capture it.

The alternative I built uses four commercial load cells *as the structure*. Each one is
factory-characterized to carry its full rated load, so the error terms are on a datasheet
instead of in an uncharacterized flexure.

## What I designed

Four TAL221 500&nbsp;g bar load cells join two 3D-printed plates in a square. Each cell only
senses compression along the vertical axis; together they resolve **F<sub>z</sub>, M<sub>x</sub>,
and M<sub>y</sub>**. A lateral force is then recovered from the moment it makes about the cell
plane — which is why the tool's contact height has to be known (here, 38.6&nbsp;mm above the
cell mid-plane).

<Figure src="p1/cad_exploded.png" panel wide alt="Exploded view of the sensor showing the top plate, four bar load cells, converter boards, and bottom plate." caption="Only 3 of 17 part types are custom: two printed plates and an interchangeable tip. The electronics bill of materials is $116.55." />

Every load cell gets its own NAU7802 24-bit converter, interleaved through an I²C multiplexer
for about 1200&nbsp;Hz aggregate into a Teensy 4.0. A single fitted **3×4 matrix plus bias**
maps four tared counts to (F<sub>x</sub>, F<sub>y</sub>, F<sub>z</sub>) — and absorbs assembly
tolerance, cell-to-cell gain spread, wiring polarity, and cross-coupling in one step. Because
the fit is what makes the build forgiving, the mechanical design could stay simple.

<Figure src="p1/internals.jpg" alt="The four load cells mounted between the printed plates, with the converter boards and I2C multiplexer." caption="As built — the four cells are the load path; the converters sit on the perimeter and the multiplexer at the center." />

## Calibrating and validating it

I established the lab's calibration method for this class of sensor: load each axis with known
dead weights, then fit the matrix by **ridge-regularized least squares** (the four-cell system
is rank 3, not 4). For the lateral axes the sensor is clamped horizontal at the bench edge so
gravity pulls perpendicular to the axis being loaded.

<FigureRow panel={false} items={[
  { src: "p1/cal_lateral.jpg", alt: "Sensor clamped horizontally at a bench edge with a mass hanging from the tip on a string.", label: "Lateral — mass hung from the tip" },
  { src: "p1/cal_axial.jpg", alt: "A calibration mass resting on the sensor tip.", label: "Axial — mass on the tip" }
]} caption="Dead-weight calibration. Known masses beat calibrating against another sensor — no second instrument's errors enter the fit." />

For an independent check, the sensor and a commercial OptoForce rode in a serial stack on a
UR5e, so both saw the same contact force. Over **344,424 in-contact samples**, after one scale
factor per axis, the readings sat within **2.3% / 2.5% / 1.0%** of the reference (r² ≥ 0.993) —
matching or beating the closest comparable published design.

<Figure src="p1/agreement.png" panel wide alt="Three scatter panels of sensor output against the OptoForce reference, each hugging the 1:1 line." caption="Response against the reference. Dashed line is 1:1; black is a least-squares fit through the origin." />

Finally I used it to close a control loop: **admittance hand-guiding** on the UR5e at
199.8&nbsp;Hz, where the measured contact force commands the arm's velocity.

<VideoFigure src="/media/lc3-handguiding.mp4" poster="/media/lc3-handguiding.poster.jpg" ratio="3 / 4" caption="Admittance hand-guiding — pushing on the sensor drives the arm. Fast and accurate enough to run on a moving robot, not just a bench." />

Cost of the sensing electronics: **$116.55**. Overloads of 19.9–28.7&nbsp;N against a 19.6&nbsp;N
rating left the unloaded output within 0.05&nbsp;N of zero — a different load-cell rating changes
the range without changing the design.

## What I'd do differently

- **The fixed-contact-height condition is real.** Lateral force is inferred from a moment, so
  this design suits tools whose contact point is known, not arbitrary end-effectors.
- Light assembly preload showed up as a **15–20% load/unload asymmetry**. The fix is a stiffer
  mount and more preload, not more calibration data.
- The axial scale factor only reproduced between sessions to about **±9%** — most likely
  contact-height (lever-arm) variation while the arm was hand-driven during the comparison.
- Validation was one reference on one rig. A defensible specification needs several masses per
  axis, run more than once.
